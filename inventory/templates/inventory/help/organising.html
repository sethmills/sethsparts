The shape of the thing

Everything is a part. A part is the thing you'd search for by name — “M3 x 12 socket cap screw”, “DS18B20 sensor”, “1k resistor”.

A part doesn't live anywhere by itself. It has one or more stock rows, and each row says how many of it are where. That split is the single most useful thing to understand about this app, because it means the same part can be in three places at once:

  • 40 × M3 screws in drawer 12
  • 200 × the same screws in a moving box in the loft
  • 8 × the same screws in the small parts tin on the bench

Search once, and the app tells you all three locations with quantities. Search for the part name, not the drawer number.

Containers, drawers, bins

Three levels, in this order:

  • Container — a cabinet, a shelf unit, or a moving box. Numbered, and usually named. “Cabinet 1”, “Box A”.
  • Drawer — inside a container. “drawer 12”.
  • Bin — one of sixteen subdivided spots inside a drawer, four rows of four.

A sub-bin splits a bin further when a single bin of resistors is really four different values. Use them when you need them; most parts don't.

Containers have barcodes, drawers and bins can have them, and scanning either takes you straight to it.

Category is for finding, location is for fetching

A category answers “what kind of thing is this?” — Fasteners, Electronics, 3D printing. Location answers “where do I have to walk?”.

They're independent, and both matter. Categories are how you browse when you're not sure of a name. Locations are how you get the part into your hand.

Categories are also what decide sharing, if you ever turn on community search — you tick which categories another workshop may search, rather than exposing everything or nothing.

What to do every time you add a part

  1. Name it how you'd say it out loud. “M3 x 12 socket cap”, not “screw”. You'll be searching for it by the words in your head, not by what a supplier called it.
  2. Put it somewhere you'll look. A real drawer beats a logical one. If you'd never think to look in drawer 4 for it, don't put it there.
  3. Give it a category. Uncategorised parts are invisible when you browse, and invisible is nearly as bad as lost.
  4. Set a reorder threshold if running out would annoy you. The reorder page then tells you what to buy, instead of you discovering it mid-project.

Where this goes wrong

The app won't stop you doing any of these, and every one of them is a trap:

  • Putting everything in one drawer. Fine at twenty parts, miserable at two hundred. Split by the question you'll actually ask — “where are the fasteners?” — not by what fits.
  • Vague names. “Screws” is unusable once you have six kinds. The name is the search key, so make it specific.
  • Skipping the location. A part with no location is a part you own and can't find, which is the problem this app exists to solve.
  • Recording quantities you can't keep accurate. An approximate number you trust beats an exact one you don't. Where a count is genuinely fuzzy, put the rough figure in and move on — this is an inventory, not an audit.
  • Never pruning. Old projects’ leftover stock accumulates. The reorder page filters by category, so a periodic look is worth more than any schema.

A layout that works

One arrangement that survives contact with a real workshop, in case it's useful as a starting point:

  • Cabinet 1 — fasteners and hardware. Drawers by type: machine screws, wood screws, washers, nuts, inserts, misc.
  • Cabinet 2 — electronics. Passive components by value bands, then semiconductors, then modules.
  • Cabinet 3 — tools, adhesives, consumables, and the things that don't fit a category.
  • Moving boxes — bulk and overflow, with their real location in the name (“Box A — loft”).

The point isn't this exact layout. It's that each drawer answers one question, and you'd know where to look without the app's help — because then the app is a lookup rather than a lifeline.
