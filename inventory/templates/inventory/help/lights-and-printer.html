Both are optional, and both are expected to be missing

This app runs perfectly well with no lights and no printer. Those features appear when you configure an address for them and stay hidden until you do — so nobody gets a button that only ever fails.

The app never touches hardware directly. It talks to a small service running on a Raspberry Pi next to the hardware, over your network. That's why the same app runs in a container, on a laptop, or on a Pi.

The LED “find the part” indicator

A strip of addressable LEDs along each side of a cabinet. Ask the app where a part is, and the relevant drawer lights up.

Two pieces: the app stores which LEDs belong to which drawer, and the Pi stores which strip is wired to which output. The app can't know the second part — that's physical wiring you can see and it can't.

So when you press find the part and nothing lights:

  1. Does the connection test on the settings page pass? If not, the Pi or the address is the problem.
  2. Does the drawer have an LED mapping? Without one there's nothing to light, and the app says so.
  3. Is the strip named in the mapping present in the Pi's strip_map.json? That file maps a strip name to a physical channel.

If all three pass and the strips still stay dark, suspect the board itself. The quickest test is Settings → Flash the LED controller, which asks the Pi to run the board's demo — that only works if firmware is actually running on it. The same page walks through installing that firmware, including the one that catches everybody: the board needs a true hardware reset after its first boot, because a soft reset never brings up the second serial channel.

A drawer usually has two mappings — one per side of the cabinet — and both light together. Within a drawer, one strip shows the row and the other shows the column, so a bin's position reads across the two.

The label printer

The app renders a label as an image and sends the finished bytes to a print bridge on the Pi, which relays them to the printer over USB. Nothing needs a driver on the machine running the app. The bridge writes raw printer bytes straight to the device node, or hands the image to CUPS if your printer type is the CUPS one.

Pick your printer's language on the settings page. Zebra (ZPL) is the only one that has been tested against real hardware — this app was built around a Zebra GK420T, and every label it prints goes that way. Brother QL, Dymo and a generic CUPS route are implemented from the manufacturers' own documentation and have never been tried on the printers they target. They may well work; nobody has proved it. The settings page says the same thing next to each option.

A printer that answers a health check but prints nothing is the common failure, which is why the test button actually sends a label rather than pinging it.

Matching labels to the stock you buy

Label sizes are defined in inventory/label_printing.py as LABEL_SIZES — a name, a display label, and width and height in inches. The three defaults are the sizes this was built around.

Change that dictionary to match what you actually buy and every label in the app follows. The resolution comes from your printer type, or from the dots-per-inch box on the settings page if your printer is the 300dpi version of a 203dpi design (which most of them are sold as). Get it wrong and labels print the right shape at the wrong size.

A print head is a fixed width — 832 dots on a GK420T, 720 on a Brother QL, 448 on a Dymo — so a label wider than that cannot be printed at all. The settings page shows which of your label sizes fit, and the app refuses rather than sending something that would come out clipped.

Using it without the lights

Everything else still works: search, browse, scanning, labels if you have a printer, projects, reorder. The “find the part” button simply isn't offered.

The barcode on the drawer is doing most of the work anyway — scanning it takes you to the same place the lights would have pointed at, and needs no wiring at all.
